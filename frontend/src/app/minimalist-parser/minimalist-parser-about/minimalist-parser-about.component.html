Overview

Computational linguistics and mainstream linguistics are once again diverging. Very large language models such as GPT have mysteriously cracked the code of English grammar, consistently generating grammatical sentences despite never having explicitly been taught grammar. While these advances are amazing, they leave the basic questions of linguistics unanswered: what it language like? Why can babies learn it so automatically with orders of magnitude less input than GPT?

LLMs learn the language globally, based on indiscriminate input. Linguists, on the other hand, often aim to describe accurately small corners of the grammar of a language: what explains the order of adjectives in a noun phrase? Why can any appear in only certain contexts? How do verbs get their suffixes? While the overall aim is to provide a complete picture of how human languages can work and what makes each language different, often these small components are not in practice put together in to a complete grammar. As a result, it is not always clear what proposals are mutually compatible.

This is where computational models can help. Computational models force us to make very specific decisions. We can try putting together many researchers' ideas into a large grammar of many aspects of a language, and see whether the model still makes sense.

We can also use machine learning to teach a model what these grammatical structures can look like, and see whether they can make sense of new sentences and structures.

This project is in this space, using modern machine learning tools to address the question of how language actually works.

Specifically, this is a parser: a model that automatically generates a linguistic structure for a sentence. The kind of linguistic structure generated is a Minimalist derivation tree, which is a description of the building process of a sentence, using contemporary syntactic theory.

The data for this project is from MGBank (Torr 2018), a corpus of about 80,000 minimalist derivation trees for sentences mostly taken from the Penn Tree Bank.

The visualisations are powered by Jonas Groschwitz's Vulcan, an open-source visualisation tool for linguistic structures.

This work is part of the research programme Learning meaning from structure: neural semantic parsing with minimalist grammars with project number VI.Veni.194.057, which is funded by the Dutch Research Council (NWO).

Minimalist syntax

Much of comtemporary syntactic theory is dominated by Noam Chomsky's Minimalist Program (Chomsky 1995 etc), loosely defined by theories of grammar that rely on the very simple idea that you build up a sentence by repeatedly taking two things and putting them together. Which things you put together and in which order determine both the form of the sentence—the order of its words—and the meaning of the sentence.

For example, the sentence Every good boy deserves fudge can be built by putting boy and good together to make good boy, then adding every, creating the subject of the sentence every good boy.

Meanwhile deserves merges with its object fudge, forming the predicate deserves fudge. Only then can we put the whole sentence together, merge predicate with subject. Such a procedure can be represented with a derivation tree, as follows:

Minimalist Grammars (Stabler 1996, etc.)

Minimalist Grammars (MGs) are a formalisation of some of the ideas behind Chomsky's Minimalist Program. Formalising requires making very specific choices about the design of a system. For example, while in Minimalism, it is generally thought that each word is a bundle of features that, among other things, drive at least some aspects of the derivation, in MGs, each word comes with a stack of features, and the features determine which operations are possible and what they do.

For example, deserves is a transitive verb, meaning it requires both a subject and an object. This is expressed in features on the verb, requesting two nouns: deserves::=N =N V.

Shift-reduce parsing and Recurrent Neural Network Grammars (RNNGs)

Parsing is the task of automatically generating a grammatical structure for a sentence. In my case, this is a minimalist derivation tree (see above), which constitutes building instructions for the sentence.

Dyer et al (2016) created a grammar/parser that uses neural networks to predict syntax trees for sentences, step by step. For instance, one step could be to put good and boy together to form a noun phrase. A neural network is used to encode both the sentence and the current state of the parser—the trees built so far, the steps taken to do so, what words remain to be handled, etc. To get it to work, they trained it on thousands of examples of sentences and syntax trees until it was very good at predicting the process of building the trees for the sentences they had. The hope is then that it will also work well for new sentences.

The trees of interest for Dyer et al are phrase structure trees, in which the internal nodes are labeled with names for the type of the subtrees they are above. For example, good boy is a noun phrase (NP) made out of a noun (N) and an adjective (A):

Kuncoro et al (2017) found that the method was not only good at generating trees for new sentences, but it was also good at it even when the internal nodes of the tree were not labelled, and there were no rules about which types of trees could be combined with which other ones. That is, it worked almost as well when there was no explicit grammar at all. Somehow the model was able to implicitly learn some approximation of the grammar rules and put together words and trees just based on the network's encoding of those trees. An example of a tree without internal node labels is below.

The advantage of not having rules about what is allowed to combine with what is that we as linguists don't actually know all the rules of any language yet. Constraining your model with an incomplete set of rules means it can't handle any kinds of sentences that you the linguist don't know how to handle. Having the model implicitly learn something like the grammar is harder, but also much more flexible.

Research Question

My question was this: can something similar work for Minimalism? Could we leave out the features altogether, and train a model like the one in Kuncoro et al (2017) to generate featureless Minimalist derivation trees?

A major challenge for Minimalism is that while for phrase structure trees, the order of the words in the sentence is the same as the order of words in the tree, in Minimalism this is not always true. This is because Minimalism, in additional to Merge, which puts two things together, also has Move, which takes something already Merged and puts it in a different place in the sentence. For example, what in the sentence below is thought to start in the usual object position after deserves, just like fudge in every good boy deserves fudge, but then later Moves to the start of the sentence.

What does every good boy deserve?

The derivation tree for this sentence would look something like this. Notice that what is not at the beginning of the sentence in the tree. Instead it appears where it was Merged. The Move node at the top is the instruction to move what to the beginning of the sentence.

The model in this demo

This research is still ongoing. This demo is not exactly the RNNG-inspired model, but rather a strong baseline—a model that uses most of the relevant ideas, but does not yet include the special ingredient that addresses the exact question of interest.

While a future model will use the RNNG-style parser, which generates the tree step by step, using an encoding of all the partially-constructed trees to guide its decision, the version here is a type of "sequence-to-sequence" model.

Specifically, this is the BART language model, fine-tuned to directly generate derivation trees as strings. For example, given What does every good boy deserve? its job is to generate, one element at a time, including the brackets and commas:

[ Move [ Merge does , [ Merge [ Merge every , [ Merge good , boy ] ] [ Merge deserve , what ] ] ] ]

It works suprisingly well. Around 75% of sequences like this are in fact interpretable as derivation trees.

Minimalist operations in this corpus

Because my derivation trees have no features, the operations need to be split up into more than just Merge and Move.

Merge operations

There are two kinds of Merge operations:

Merge 1
Concatenate the selected phrase to the left or right of the selector
Merge 2

Merge a word that will later move. A maximum of 4 phrases can be waiting to move at a time:

  • A-movers (e.g. for case)
  • A'-movers (e.g. wh-movement)
  • R-movers (rightward movers, usually for pragmatic, prosodic, or coordination reasons)
  • Self-movers

There is one Merge 2 function for each of the four mover slots.

Additionally, MGBank allows for Head Movement. For example, in Does every good boy deserve fudge? the auxilliary does is thought to start in the T(ense) position and move up to the C(omplementiser) position. Rather than the whole TP constituent moving, only the T head itself moves:

In Minimalist grammars, this is modelled as a variant of Merge. In my featureless Minimalist algebras, there is a Merge operation for each kind of head movement (left, right, excorporation, and across-the-board). It updates the inner algebra term. (Those without any head movement are annotated with "simple" in the operation name).

For example, Merge1+concat_right+simple simply concatenates the selectee to the right of the selector: Merge1+concat_right+simple(deserves, fudge) = deserves fudge.

Merge2+Abar, on the other hand, stores the selectee in the Abar mover slot, to be Moved later:

Merge2+Abar(deserve, what) = (deserve, {{ "{" }}Abar:what{{ "}" }})

Move operations

There are two kinds of Move operations:

Move 1 (final move)
concatenate the mover to the main phrase
Move 2 (nonfinal move)
The mover will move again, so do not concatenate it. Refile it in its new mover slot.

There is a Move 1 for each of the four mover slots (A, A', R, Self), indicating which slots to get the mover from. There is a Move 2 for each pair of mover slots, indicating which slot to take the mover from and where to refile it.

For example, Move2+A+Abar takes whatever is stored in the A mover slot and refiles it in the Abar mover slot: Move2+A+Bar((deserves fudge, {{ "{" }}A:who{{ "}" }})) = (deserves fudge, {{ "{" }}Abar:who{{ "}" }})

Move1+concat_left+Abar, on the other hand, takes whatever is stored in the Abar mover slot and concatenates it to the left: Move1+concat_left+Abar((deserves fudge, {{ "{" }}Abar:who{{ "}" }})) = who deserves fudge.
